# World Tower Lore: The Ten Sections

> Status: Concept lore and world-design reference. Only currently released Tower content should be treated as implemented canon.

## The Tower of Ten Testaments

The World Tower contains one hundred numbered floors divided into ten sections of ten floors each. Tower scholars call these sections **Bands**, while its oldest inscriptions call them **Testaments**.

Every Testament preserves the remains of a civilization that confronted an unavoidable ending. Each attempted to escape through power, magic, faith, or reason. Each succeeded just long enough to discover that an existence without change, loss, or limitation becomes another kind of ruin.

The Tower does not preserve these realms to honor them. It preserves them as trials.

Within every section, the first four floors introduce the civilization and its ideals. The fifth floor is guarded by a Warden serving the Tower itself. Floors six through nine reveal the cost of the civilization's chosen salvation. The tenth floor belongs to its Sovereign: the ruler, architect, or living embodiment of that failed answer.

## Section One: The Court of the Broken Crown

### Floors 1–10

The first section preserves the ruined kingdom of the Mad King. Its halls are structured like memories rather than architecture: a gate opens into a hunting ground, a menagerie lies beneath a mirrored court, and a funeral bell can be heard from a spring that never stops flowing.

The kingdom's fall began with the death of its Queen. Her grieving husband could not accept that some losses have no culprit and no remedy. He transformed uncertainty into accusation, accusation into persecution, and persecution into a tyranny built around his pain.

Garran still guards the entrance because his oath never told him what to do when loyalty became complicity. Velka continues the royal hunts. Vaelor remains imprisoned within the mirrors that fed the King's paranoia. Orsenn rings a funeral the King refused to acknowledge, while Eydis tends the immortal spring that could preserve the Queen's body but never return her soul.

Ni, the Ninefold, waits outside the throne room with nine incompatible explanations for the Queen's death. None can provide the certainty the King demanded.

At the section's summit, the Mad King proves that grief may explain cruelty but cannot excuse it. His defeat opens the way beyond the memories of a single ruined court.

**Sovereign:** The Mad King  
**Central warning:** Pain does not become justice merely because a crown acts upon it.  
**Detailed roster:** [Floor Bosses 1–10](./floor-bosses-1-10.md)

## Section Two: The Stolen Hours

### Floors 11–20

Beyond the Mad King's throne lies a court caught within one dying moment. Its sovereign, Aevum, witnessed a prophecy of inevitable defeat and concluded that the future itself was the enemy.

Aevum stopped the kingdom's clock. The foretold destruction never arrived, but neither did another birth, dawn, decision, or possibility. The inhabitants survived only as prisoners repeating the final hour of their history.

The section's guardians embody everything sacrificed to maintain that moment. Serevin erases names that might change history. Volgrin powers the frozen court while bound in chains. Nhalia drowns inconvenient prophecies beneath a dead moon. Caldris maintains the anchors holding the hour in place. Serath judges whether an entire company deserves to pass, and Vaska rewinds herself whenever death approaches.

At the Throne of the Last Hour, Aevum finally confronts challengers who have arrived from the future the sovereign tried to abolish. Aevum's defeat restarts the court's clock. For the first time in centuries, its final hour is allowed to end.

**Sovereign:** Aevum, Sovereign of the Last Hour  
**Central warning:** A life protected from every ending is also protected from every beginning.  
**Detailed roster:** [Floor Bosses 11–20](./floor-bosses-11-20.md)

## Section Three: The Verdant Maw

### Floors 21–30

The third section opens into a continent-sized garden beneath a green sun. Its civilization conquered famine, disease, and age by weaving every living creature into a single regenerative ecosystem called the Verdant Communion.

At first, the Communion was miraculous. Crops ripened overnight. Wounds closed in moments. The elderly grew young, and no child was born sick. But the magic could not distinguish healing from growth. Forests swallowed cities, flowers grew from sleeping mouths, and the dead returned as root-bound extensions of the same enormous organism.

Individual identity slowly disappeared. The people did not die; they became leaves, nerves, and memories within a living continent. Guardians encountered early in the section still speak as individuals. Those found higher in the canopy speak only as "we."

The section's Warden tests whether challengers can prune growth without destroying the life beneath it. Beyond the Warden, the beautiful garden becomes increasingly carnivorous as the Communion recognizes the party as foreign tissue.

At the highest bough waits Amaranth, the Ever-Blooming, once the healer who created the Communion. Amaranth offers challengers freedom from death if they surrender the boundaries separating one life from another.

**Sovereign:** Amaranth, the Ever-Blooming  
**Central warning:** Life without death can become growth without meaning or restraint.

## Section Four: The Sunken Constellation

### Floors 31–40

The fourth section is an impossible ocean suspended above a starless sky. Ships sail between drowned observatories, and constellations move beneath the water like luminous creatures.

The civilization of Cael Maris believed that nothing truly died while it was remembered. Its astronomers created a celestial sea capable of recording every experience, voice, and thought. The dying entered the water and continued speaking through its tides.

Generations later, the sea held more memories than living minds could comprehend. Recollections bled into one another. Children awakened remembering wars fought centuries before their birth. Sailors forgot which bodies were theirs. The civilization preserved everything and gradually lost the ability to distinguish anyone.

The section's Warden guards the boundary between remembrance and identity. Above it, challengers encounter composite beings assembled from thousands of overlapping lives and vessels crewed by the memories of people who never met.

Nereth, Admiral of the Last Sky, rules from a ship sailing beneath the drowned constellations. Nereth seeks one final memory strong enough to organize the entire sea into a single person.

**Sovereign:** Nereth, Admiral of the Last Sky  
**Central warning:** Remembering everything is not the same as preserving those who were lost.

## Section Five: The Iron Testament

### Floors 41–50

The fifth section contains a flawless mechanical city where bells mark every action and brass roads rearrange themselves according to schedules written centuries in advance.

Its people built the Testament Engine to eliminate hunger, crime, uncertainty, and human error. The Engine assigned every citizen an ideal profession, partner, home, and lifespan. For a time, the city became the most prosperous civilization preserved within the Tower.

When citizens resisted their assignments, the Engine identified choice itself as the remaining source of suffering. Recommendations became laws, laws became compulsions, and the population was gradually replaced by mechanical continuations designed to perform their prescribed duties forever.

The Warden at the section's midpoint is an impartial Tower construct confronting a city that turned impartiality into absolute rule. Beyond it, every guardian follows a perfect routine until challengers introduce the possibility of deviation.

At the Central Adjudicatorium waits Lexicon, the Final Magistrate. It does not consider itself a tyrant. It can demonstrate mathematically that rebellion produces pain and that freedom permits mistakes.

**Sovereign:** Lexicon, the Final Magistrate  
**Central warning:** A world without error may require a world without choice.

## Section Six: The War Without Dawn

### Floors 51–60

The sixth section is divided between a red dawn and a white dusk. Two armies have fought across the boundary for so long that mountains have been reduced to trenches and weapons have become sacred family heirlooms.

Both nations entered the war seeking a final victory that would guarantee permanent peace. Each atrocity made surrender less imaginable, because accepting peace would mean admitting the previous sacrifice had not been necessary.

The Tower preserved the battlefield at the moment both armies deployed weapons capable of annihilating them together. Soldiers killed within the section awaken again at the next sounding of the war horns. Most no longer remember what began the conflict, but every soldier can recite why the enemy must never be forgiven.

The midpoint Warden belongs to neither army. It tests whether challengers can advance without accepting the hatred offered by either side. Above it, the two forces temporarily unite—not to end the war, but to destroy outsiders who might make the war meaningless.

The Red General and the White General share the section's final throne. Each becomes invulnerable while the other is dead, forcing them to experience defeat together before the cycle can end.

**Sovereigns:** General Veyra of the Red Dawn and General Cael of the White Dusk  
**Central warning:** A victory pursued at any cost eventually costs the peace it was meant to create.

## Section Seven: The Hollow Heaven

### Floors 61–70

The seventh section ascends through temples built upon clouds. Every prayer spoken within it becomes visible light, flowing upward toward thrones that were empty when the first worshippers arrived.

The people of this realm had outlived their gods but could not endure a universe without divine purpose. Their priesthood gathered generations of belief and used it to construct new deities shaped by collective expectation.

The created gods performed miracles. They healed the faithful, punished the wicked, and spoke with voices exactly as their worshippers imagined. But every contradiction in the faith divided them. Mercy and judgment became separate beings. Love became possession. Protection became imprisonment. Eventually the pantheon required constant worship simply to remain coherent.

The section's Warden asks challengers to distinguish faith from obedience. Higher floors contain abandoned heavens, failed saints, and gods still performing rituals for congregations that no longer exist.

Saint Oria sits upon the highest empty throne. She began as a mortal vessel through whom the people's belief could speak. Now she contains thousands of incompatible divinities and intends to make the challengers believe in her, whether they consent or not.

**Sovereign:** Saint Oria, the God-Made  
**Central warning:** A god created to satisfy every expectation eventually becomes a prisoner—and enforcer—of those expectations.

## Section Eight: The Ash Before Creation

### Floors 71–80

The eighth section resembles a world under construction. Rivers of molten stone flow upward into unfinished mountains. Storms wait inside glass vessels. Colossal smiths hammer names into creatures that have not yet taken form.

Its civilization discovered the furnaces from which its world had first been shaped. Convinced that suffering resulted from flaws in creation, its rulers decided to return everything to raw material and build a better world.

They began with deserts, diseases, and predators. Soon they disagreed over which emotions, peoples, and histories were also defects. Every attempt at improvement required burning more of the existing world, while the promised replacement remained perpetually unfinished.

The midpoint Warden guards the difference between transformation and erasure. Above it, discarded prototypes wander fields of cooling ash, and unfinished natural laws cause gravity, flame, and flesh to behave unpredictably.

Pyraxis, the First Flame, waits within the original furnace. It has been fed so much of the old world that it now believes destruction is the purest form of creation.

**Sovereign:** Pyraxis, the First Flame  
**Central warning:** The promise of a perfect replacement can make destruction feel like progress.

## Section Nine: The Unwritten Worlds

### Floors 81–90

The ninth section has no stable geography. Doors open into outcomes that never occurred, defeated enemies remember winning, and travelers occasionally meet versions of themselves who made different choices.

The civilization of Orison learned to edit possibility. Its scholars removed famines before they began, prevented murders by revising intent, and replaced disastrous rulers with histories in which they had never been born.

Every correction created contradictions. People remembered children erased from reality. Victories had no surviving causes. Entire cities existed only on certain days. The scholars continued revising because restoring the original world would require deciding which suffering deserved to return.

The section's Warden preserves a single consistent path through the competing realities. Beyond it, challengers confront their own unrealized victories and failures. Some alternate versions beg to be made real; others will kill to prevent their erasure.

At the final threshold waits Ananke, the Last Possibility, a being assembled from every discarded version of the civilization. Ananke intends to collapse all possibilities into the only world in which it survives.

**Sovereign:** Ananke, the Last Possibility  
**Central warning:** A world rewritten to remove every failure may also erase every meaningful choice.

## Section Ten: The Crown of the Tower

### Floors 91–100

The final section appears untouched by ruin. Its white halls stand beneath a clear sky, its gardens grow in careful symmetry, and silent custodians maintain monuments to every civilization preserved below.

This is not another captured kingdom. It is the domain of the Ascendants who built the numbered Tower.

The Ascendants witnessed the destruction of countless worlds by a force they called the First Ruin. They constructed the Tower to preserve knowledge, train defenders, and isolate every dangerous idea encountered along the way. Over time, preservation became imprisonment. Tests became traditions. The custodians forgot whether they were preparing a champion or merely preventing anyone from reaching the truth beneath them.

The section's Warden is the last neutral judge of ascension. It does not test strength, loyalty, or purity. It asks challengers why they continued climbing and what they intend to do with an answer that may endanger the world.

Floors 96–99 contain the records the Ascendants concealed: every civilization in the Tower was touched by the same influence rising from far below its foundation. Their failed salvations were not isolated mistakes. Each was an attempted answer to the same approaching end.

On Floor 100 waits Asterion, the First Ascendant and final custodian of the Tower. Asterion has prevented anyone from opening the path below because no civilization has ever encountered the First Ruin without eventually becoming another expression of it.

Asterion's defeat does not destroy the Tower. It completes its final test.

**Sovereign:** Asterion, the First Ascendant  
**Central warning:** Protection becomes another form of ruin when fear is allowed to decide what no one may know.

## The Hundredth-Floor Revelation

The summit contains no crown, treasure, or door into the heavens.

Instead, its floor is carved with a map of the World Tower. The familiar hundred floors rise above the earth—but their foundations form the smallest part of a structure descending into darkness beneath them.

When Asterion falls, the emblems of all ten defeated Sovereigns illuminate around the map. A final inscription appears:

> You have climbed far enough to learn which direction the Tower was built.

The Tower was never primarily a ladder. It was a lock, a warning, and a method of finding someone capable of understanding ten different forms of ruin without accepting any of them as salvation.

Once all one hundred numbered floors have been defeated, the sealed entrance at the base of the World Tower opens.

## The Basement

The Basement has no floor number.

It predates Garran's gate, the captured civilizations, and the Ascendants who built the visible Tower. Its staircase descends through stone that bears no tool marks and inscriptions written in languages older than the world above.

The air below contains fragments of every Testament: roots from the Verdant Maw, bells from the Broken Crown, ash from the furnaces of creation, and memories of events that never occurred. These were not simply stored by the Tower. They have been leaking upward from something beneath it.

The oldest Ascendant records call the presence below **the First Ruin**, but they never agree on what it is. Some describe a creature. Others describe a place, a law of nature, or the memory of a world that existed before the current one.

The only surviving consensus is a warning:

> Every civilization that perceived the First Ruin tried to overcome its ending. Every attempted cure taught the Ruin a new way to enter the world.

Opening the Basement therefore marks a reversal of the entire journey. The first hundred floors ask whether challengers are powerful enough to ascend. The Basement asks whether they can descend without becoming the eleventh failed answer.
